🧠 Core Context: Descartes’ Method

Descartes' foundational claim—"Cogito, ergo sum"—was part of a broader rationalist project to ground knowledge in certainty. His method involved:

    Radical Doubt: Rejecting anything that could possibly be false.

    Clear and Distinct Perceptions: As criteria of truth.

🧩 Leibniz’s Critique in Detail
1. The Problem of Arbitrary Premises (Your Point)

Leibniz was wary of accepting internally consistent ideas as necessarily true without a grounding principle. He argued that:

    Internal consistency is not sufficient for truth.

    Something can be logically coherent yet metaphysically false.

    For example, a fiction or a mathematical system can be internally consistent but not describe the real world.

🔍 Leibniz’s Demand:

    Ideas must not only be logically coherent, but also possible in the divine intellect—that is, logically, metaphysically, and ethically coherent within the most rational possible world.

This is part of his Principle of Sufficient Reason:

    Nothing happens without a reason why it should be so rather than otherwise.

So, in contrast to Descartes, Leibniz insists that a premise isn't acceptable just because it’s clear and distinct or internally non-contradictory—it must have a sufficient reason in the metaphysical order.
2. Descartes’ Substance Dualism vs. Leibniz’s Monadology

Descartes postulated two substances—mind and body—that interact. But he couldn't satisfactorily explain how two fundamentally different substances could causally interact.

Leibniz rejected this interactionism. He claimed:

    There is only one kind of substance: the monad, a metaphysical point of force or perception.

    Mind and body do not interact causally but operate in pre-established harmony, arranged by God.

Thus, Descartes' reliance on dual substances was, for Leibniz, an arbitrary division that raised more metaphysical problems than it solved.
3. Descartes’ Reliance on God as Guarantor of Truth

Descartes relies heavily on the benevolence of God to justify why clear and distinct perceptions must be true (i.e., God would not deceive us).

Leibniz objected:

    This is circular reasoning (cartesian circle): you need to trust your clear and distinct ideas to prove God, but you need God to trust your ideas.

    More importantly, truth shouldn't depend on divine voluntarism, but on logical necessity.

🔍 Leibniz’s Solution:
He reinterprets God not as the mere guarantor of truth, but as a necessary being who acts in accordance with reason. Truth is eternal, even for God.
4. Descartes’ Underdeveloped Logic and Ontology

Descartes treated logic mostly informally. He didn’t develop a formal system of logic or metaphysics beyond his dualistic framework.

Leibniz, by contrast:

    Developed early ideas of formal logic (precursor to symbolic logic).

    Introduced possible worlds semantics, long before modern modal logic.

    Had a deep concern with systematic completeness—an entire metaphysical architecture, not just fragments.

Thus, for Leibniz, Descartes' system seemed piecemeal, rhetorical, and insufficiently rigorous.

🧩 What is Category Theory?

Category theory is a mathematics of relationships, not objects. It studies not the elements inside structures, but how entire structures relate to one another through mappings.

Where other branches of mathematics ask "What is this?", category theory asks:

    “How do these things relate via structure-preserving transformations?”

At its core, a category consists of:

    Objects (which could be sets, spaces, types, etc.)

    Morphisms (aka arrows): structure-preserving functions between objects

    A notion of composition: if there’s a morphism from A to B, and from B to C, you can compose them into a morphism from A to C

    An identity morphism for every object (like a "do nothing" transformation)

It’s like a universal grammar for all structured systems.
📦 So What’s a Functor?

A functor is a kind of mapping between categories that preserves structure.

    It maps objects to objects

    And maps morphisms to morphisms

    While preserving identity and composition

Think of it like a translator between structured languages—it tells you how to convert objects and processes from one context into another without losing meaning.
🔄 Now, What is an Endofunctor?

“Endo” means within.
So an endofunctor is a functor that maps a category back into itself.

In simpler terms:

    It takes objects and arrows from a category (say, the category of types in programming),

    And returns new objects and arrows inside the same category,

    Following consistent rules.

This is the engine of recursive structure: you operate on your own category within itself.
🎓 In Programming Terms:

In functional programming (like Haskell), an endofunctor is a type constructor that:

    Takes a type A and returns a new type F A

    Maps functions A → B to F A → F B

For example:

    The List functor maps Int to [Int], and a function Int → String to a function [Int] → [String].

    It “lifts” transformations into the context of a list.

This is exactly what happens in monads. A monad is an endofunctor with two extra operations:

    unit (aka return) — injects a value into the monadic context
